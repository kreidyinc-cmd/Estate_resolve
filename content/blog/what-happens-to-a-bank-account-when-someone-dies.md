When a UK bank is told that an account holder has died, it will usually freeze withdrawals from accounts in that person's sole name. A surviving joint holder can generally continue using a joint account, although ownership needs particular care in Scotland. Probate is not always required to release bank funds: each institution sets its own requirements. [MoneyHelper explains the difference between individual and joint accounts](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/family-and-care/death-and-bereavement/dealing-with-finances-and-insurance-after-your-partner-dies), and [GOV.UK explains when probate may be needed](https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate).

If you are dealing with the estate, start by locating the will and account records, contacting each known bank's bereavement team, and identifying essential payments that might be interrupted. Ask what evidence the bank needs, whether it can pay a funeral invoice, and how to obtain balances at the date of death. Keep a record of each enquiry and response.

## What happens when a bank is notified of a death?

Notification starts the bank's bereavement process. It does not immediately close every account or release the money to relatives. The bank checks the circumstances, explains its document requirements and restricts access where appropriate.

For example, [HSBC's bereavement guidance](https://www.hsbc.co.uk/help/life-events/bereavement/what-to-do-when-someone-dies/) says sole accounts are usually frozen against withdrawals, although money can still be paid in. Ask the relevant bank how it handles incoming payments, cards and online access; another institution's procedure may differ.

**Notifying a bank and having authority to administer the estate are separate matters.** The person reporting the death may not be the person entitled to collect the funds. Ask the bank who it can deal with and what evidence of authority it requires.

## Sole versus joint bank accounts after death

A sole account is in one person's name. Its balance normally needs to be dealt with as part of that person's estate. A joint account has more than one holder, so the surviving holder's access and rights must also be considered.

At a glance:

- **Access:** sole-account withdrawals are usually frozen after notification; a surviving joint holder can generally continue using the account, subject to the bank's arrangements.
- **Regular payments:** payments from a sole account may stop; payments from a continuing joint account generally carry on.
- **Release of funds:** a sole account may require probate or other evidence of authority; a joint account passing to a survivor may not require a grant for that account.
- **Ownership:** sole-account money normally belongs to the estate; establish the ownership of joint money, especially where Scottish law applies.

For England, [Citizens Advice explains that couples' joint-account money normally passes to the surviving partner without probate](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/family/death-and-wills/dealing-with-the-financial-affairs-of-someone-who-has-died/). A grant may still be needed for other estate assets. Do not assume that access to a joint account resolves every question about who owns the balance.

## What happens to a joint account in Scotland?

In Scotland, the surviving holder does not automatically own all the money simply because the account is joint. The bank's mandate governs access, while beneficial ownership concerns who is entitled to the money.

[Citizens Advice Scotland explains the joint-account rules](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/scotland/family/death-and-wills/after-death-dealing-with-an-estate/). For spouses or civil partners who both contributed, equal ownership is presumed; contributions from only one person can change the position. For other joint holders, the executor must establish the deceased's share. The surviving holder may still have access under the mandate.

Keep evidence of contributions and account arrangements. If ownership is unclear, get Scottish legal advice before treating the whole balance as the survivor's money or distributing it as part of the estate.

## Does a bank always require probate?

No. There is **no universal probate release threshold across UK banks**. One institution may release funds under its bereavement procedure while another requires a grant. A small balance does not guarantee release without one. [Citizens Advice describes how institutions' release policies differ](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/family/death-and-wills/dealing-with-the-financial-affairs-of-someone-who-has-died/).

In England and Wales, a grant of probate is normally used where an executor acts under a valid will; letters of administration are generally used where there is no will. [GOV.UK's probate guide](https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate) explains eligibility and tells you to check with each financial organisation.

Scotland uses **confirmation**; the [Scottish Courts and Tribunals Service explains the process](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/taking-action/dealing-with-a-deceaseds-estate-in-scotland/). Northern Ireland has its own [probate guidance on nidirect](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/probate).

Ask each bank to confirm its requirements for this estate. A bank agreeing to release its balance does not decide whether a grant is needed for the estate's other assets.

## What documents will the bank request?

Requirements vary, but prepare to provide:

- The death certificate or other evidence of death the bank accepts.
- Details of the deceased and any known accounts.
- The will, if there is one, and information about who is dealing with the estate.
- Identification and proof of address for the person acting.
- Probate, letters of administration or confirmation, if required.
- The bank's own bereavement or release forms.

[HSBC's document checklist](https://www.hsbc.co.uk/help/life-events/bereavement/what-to-do-when-someone-dies/) is a useful example, but use the relevant bank's instructions. Ask which documents it needs to provide information and which it needs to release money. Confirm whether it accepts uploads, copies or originals before sending documents.

## Can funeral expenses be paid before probate?

Often, a bank can consider paying a funeral bill from the deceased's account before a grant is issued. This is a specific bereavement arrangement, not general permission to withdraw money. The institution's policy and available funds matter. [Citizens Advice describes this exception](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/family/death-and-wills/dealing-with-the-financial-affairs-of-someone-who-has-died/).

For example, [HSBC says it can arrange a funeral payment after receiving formal confirmation of death and a copy of the bill](https://www.hsbc.co.uk/help/life-events/bereavement/what-to-do-when-someone-dies/). Ask whether your bank will pay the funeral director directly, what paperwork it needs and whether it can cover the amount. Do not assume it will reimburse an expense you have already paid.

## What happens to standing orders and Direct Debits?

Payments from a frozen sole account may stop. Direct Debits from a joint account that remains open generally continue. [MoneyHelper's guidance on household bills after bereavement](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/family-and-care/death-and-bereavement/dealing-with-finances-and-insurance-after-your-partner-dies) explains why payment arrangements may need updating.

Make a list of regular payments and ask the bank what has stopped or will continue. Contact the relevant suppliers about essential bills, insurance and subscriptions. Stopping a payment instruction does not itself settle an outstanding bill or cancel a contract. For a mortgage, [GOV.UK recommends asking the lender whether payments must continue while probate is being arranged](https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/identify-assets-debts).

## How to find unknown or forgotten bank accounts

Start with records you can lawfully access: statements, savings passbooks, letters, tax papers and correspondence with financial advisers. Look for transfers to another bank, previous addresses and older names used by the deceased. Record the provider, the clue and the next enquiry rather than treating a reference as proof of an open account.

### Contact known providers first

If you know the bank or building society, contact its bereavement team directly. [My Lost Account's guidance for executors](https://www.mylostaccount.org.uk/faqs) recommends this because the account may still be active, rather than classified as lost. For known NS&I holdings, contact NS&I directly.

### Use free tracing for lost accounts

[My Lost Account provides a free tracing service](https://www.mylostaccount.org.uk/start-search) for lost UK personal bank, building society and NS&I accounts. Supply as much identifying information as possible, including previous names, addresses and the date of death. It is not a search for every pension, investment, business account or overseas asset.

For a broader record-checking approach, our guide to [finding a deceased person's assets](/blog/find-assets-deceased-person) covers enquiries beyond bank accounts.

## Death Notification Service versus My Lost Account

These services solve different problems:

- **Death Notification Service:** a free way to notify participating organisations of a death through one submission. It can lead to contact about active accounts, including ones you did not know about. Check the current member list; it is not a search across every financial institution.
- **My Lost Account:** a free tracing route for lost or dormant personal accounts within its scope. A death notification does not replace this search where older accounts may have been forgotten.

The [Death Notification Service explains its notification process](https://www.deathnotificationservice.co.uk/), while [My Lost Account's FAQs distinguish active-account notification from dormant-account tracing](https://www.mylostaccount.org.uk/faqs). Neither service grants authority to administer the estate or bypasses a provider's checks before payment. The Death Notification Service says members do not write back if no account is held, so keep track of which enquiries still need direct follow-up.

## How executors establish account values

Request a written **date-of-death balance** from each provider. A current statement may include later transactions and therefore show a different figure. Ask about interest accrued up to death and any related debt, and retain the bank's response with the estate records.

[GOV.UK advises contacting organisations for the value of assets and debts when the person died](https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/identify-assets-debts), supplying a copy of the death certificate. These figures help establish the estate's value for administration and any tax reporting. For a joint account, establishing the deceased's share is a separate step from obtaining the total balance.

Keep three outcomes distinct:

1. **Account identified:** there is a possible financial relationship to investigate.
2. **Ownership and authority established:** the provider has the evidence it needs about the account and the person acting.
3. **Funds released:** the provider has completed its requirements and paid the money through the agreed process.

A search result is not a valuation or confirmation that money is ready to distribute.

## Common questions and practical next steps

### Does registering the death automatically tell the bank?

Do not rely on it. Tell Us Once deals with government organisations; banks must also be informed. [GOV.UK's guidance after a death](https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death) distinguishes these notifications. Contact the bank directly or check whether it participates in the Death Notification Service.

### Can I withdraw money using the deceased's card or online banking?

Use the bank's bereavement process to request access or an approved payment. Possessing a card, PIN or login is not evidence that you are entitled to take estate money. The bank needs to establish the appropriate authority before releasing funds.

### Is money in a dormant account lost forever?

Not simply because contact has been lost. [My Lost Account explains that money transferred under the dormant accounts scheme remains reclaimable by someone legally entitled to it](https://www.mylostaccount.org.uk/faqs). Finding the account is only the start: the provider still needs to verify the claim.

### What should I record as I work through the accounts?

Keep a simple enquiry log: provider, account reference, sole or joint ownership, notification date, documents requested, balance at death and outstanding action. Add separate entries for missing-account searches. This makes it easier to distinguish an unanswered enquiry from an account that has been checked and closed.
